## A. Insect / Drosophila anoxic coma and spreading depolarization

- Haddad et al. (1997). Genetic basis of tolerance to O 2 deprivation in Drosophila melanogaster. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 94, 10809-10812. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.94.20.10809 [Abs]  
  Genetic screen for anoxia-sensitive Drosophila mutants showing delayed recovery and prolonged polysynaptic transmission after reoxygenation; shows recovery kinetics are genetically variable.
- KRISHNAN et al. (1997). Behavioral and Electrophysiologic Responses of Drosophila melanogaster to Prolonged Periods of Anoxia. *Journal of Insect Physiology* 43, 203-210. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-1910(96)00084-4 [Abs]  
  Flies lose coordination within 1–2 min of N2, tolerate up to 4 h anoxia and recover, with a nonlinear relationship between anoxia duration and recovery time; giant-fibre-evoked muscle responses recover fully. Basis for the recovery-time-vs-duration curve.
- Rodgers et al. (2007). Stress Preconditioning of Spreading Depression in the Locust CNS. *PLoS ONE* 2, e1366. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0001366 [Rec]  
  Heat-shock and other stress preconditioning alter SD-like events in locust CNS; evidence that prior stress shifts SD threshold and recovery.
- Armstrong et al. (2009). Suppression of Spreading Depression-Like Events in Locusts by Inhibition of the NO/cGMP/PKG Pathway. *Journal of Neuroscience* 29, 8225-8235. https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1652-09.2009 [Rec]  
  NO/cGMP/PKG pathway inhibition suppresses SD-like events in locust CNS; mechanistic precedent for the PKG effect seen later in flies.
- Vigne et al. (2009). Strong Dietary Restrictions Protect Drosophila against Anoxia/Reoxygenation Injuries. *PLoS ONE* 4, e5422. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0005422 [Abs]  
  Anoxia/reoxygenation (1–6 h) in flies causes a transient mortality peak at reoxygenation, and strong dietary restriction protects. Fly-level evidence of reoxygenation injury relevant to H3.
- Rodgers et al. (2010). Coma in response to environmental stress in the locust: A model for cortical spreading depression. *Journal of Insect Physiology* 56, 980-990. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jinsphys.2010.03.030 [Abs]  
  Review of stress-induced coma in locusts as an SD model (K+ surges, propagation in neuropil, preconditioning), arguing insect SD is adaptive energy conservation. Conceptual model of SD onset/recovery.
- Armstrong et al. (2011). Glial Hsp70 Protects K+ Homeostasis in the Drosophila Brain during Repetitive Anoxic Depolarization. *PLoS ONE* 6, e28994. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0028994 [FT]  
  Ouabain-induced repetitive comas in intact flies end in a terminal [K+]o plateau (~54 mM) and death; repetitive N2 anoxia produces graded [K+]o surges, and glial Hsp70 delays permanent loss of K+ homeostasis. Source for fly [K+]o baseline/terminal values and a model of cumulative damage across repeated insults.
- Rodriguez & Robertson (2012). Protective effect of hypothermia on brain potassium homeostasis during repetitive anoxia in Drosophila melanogaster. *Journal of Experimental Biology* jeb.074468. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.074468 [Abs]  
  K+-microelectrode recordings in fly brain during repeated anoxic comas at 17, 23 and 29 °C: cooling stabilised [K+]o while warming produced large cumulative baseline drift, and the anoxia/normoxia cycling pattern itself determined damage. Most direct fly evidence bearing on H3 (reperfusion pattern) and H4 (hypothermia).
- Caraballo et al. (2014). Role of PON in Anoxia-Reoxygenation Injury: A Drosophila Melanogaster Transgenic Model. *PLoS ONE* 9, e84434. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0084434 [Abs]  
  Transgenic PON1 expression protects flies against anoxia-reoxygenation injury (1–24 h anoxia), implicating oxidative stress at reoxygenation. Supports a reperfusion oxidative-damage term.
- Hou et al. (2014). Na+-K+-ATPase trafficking induced by heat shock pretreatment correlates with increased resistance to anoxia in locusts. *Journal of Neurophysiology* 112, 814-823. https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00201.2014 [Abs]  
  Heat-shock pretreatment increases Na+/K+-ATPase α-subunit in locust neuronal membranes (~3-fold), delaying anoxic coma and speeding [K+]o clearance and recovery. Links pump density to recovery rate.
- Callier et al. (2015). Developmental changes in hypoxic exposure and responses to anoxia in Drosophila melanogaster. *Journal of Experimental Biology* jeb.125849. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.125849 [Abs]  
  Adult flies survive anoxia ~8× longer than larvae (LT50 8.3 vs 1 h) and suppress metabolic rate to ~3% of normoxia during anoxia. Survival-limit and hypometabolism parameters.
- Spong et al. (2016). Spreading depolarization in the brain of Drosophila is induced by inhibition of the Na+/K+-ATPase and mitigated by a decrease in activity of protein kinase G. *Journal of Neurophysiology* 116, 1152-1160. https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00353.2016 [Abs]  
  Establishes propagating SD in the Drosophila brain, triggered by raised [K+]o or ouabain (Na+/K+-ATPase block); older flies and w1118 are more susceptible, and low PKG lengthens latency to both ouabain SD and anoxic depolarisation. Justifies treating pump failure as the SD trigger in a fly simulator.
- Spong et al. (2016). A new direction for spreading depolarization: Investigation in the fly brain. *Channels* 11, 97-98. https://doi.org/10.1080/19336950.2016.1239898 [Rec]  
  Short commentary on the Drosophila SD preparation and its value for genetic dissection of SD mechanisms; context for the fly model.
- Spong et al. (2016). Mechanisms of spreading depolarization in vertebrate and insect central nervous systems. *Journal of Neurophysiology* 116, 1117-1127. https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00352.2016 [Rec]  
  Review comparing SD mechanisms in vertebrate and insect CNS; underpins using the fly as a proxy for mammalian anoxic depolarisation.
- Evans et al. (2017). AMP-activated protein kinase protects against anoxia in Drosophila melanogaster. *Comparative Biochemistry and Physiology Part A: Molecular & Integrative Physiology* 214, 30-39. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cbpa.2017.09.006 [Abs]  
  Starvation/AMPK activation speeds locomotor recovery from anoxic coma, and glial AMPK reduces ionic disruption during fly brain SD. Metabolic state as a modifier of recovery.
- Robertson et al. (2017). Chill coma in the locust, Locusta migratoria, is initiated by spreading depolarization in the central nervous system. *Scientific Reports* 7, 10297. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-10586-6 [Abs]  
  Chill coma in locusts is initiated by SD in the CNS; extends the SD-as-shutdown framework to cold.
- Andersen et al. (2018). Central nervous shutdown underlies acute cold tolerance in tropical and temperateDrosophilaspecies. *Journal of Experimental Biology* jeb.179598. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.179598 [FT]  
  Cold-induced SD in five Drosophila species: resting CNS [K+]o ~10 mM rising to ~30–58 mM at SD; SD temperature 5.3 °C in warm-acclimated D. melanogaster; time to anoxic SD at room temperature 14–16 s. Key source for [K+]o and anoxic SD latency.
- Campbell et al. (2018). Paralytic hypo-energetic state facilitates anoxia tolerance despite ionic imbalance in adultDrosophila melanogaster. *Journal of Experimental Biology* jeb.177147. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.177147 [FT]  
  Adult flies are paralysed within 30 s of anoxia, whole-body ATP falls to 35% by 15 min and ~3% by 30 min, then plateaus; survival stays 100% at 2 h and LT50 is ~8 h, with mortality tracking further ATP loss and [K+]o rise. Primary source for fly ATP kinetics and survival limits.
- Van Dusen et al. (2019). Role of adenosine in functional recovery following anoxic coma in Locusta migratoria. *bioRxiv (preprint)*. https://doi.org/10.1101/2019.12.19.882464 [Abs] **(preprint, not peer reviewed)**  
  Preprint (not peer reviewed): adenosine lengthens time to functional recovery after anoxic coma in locusts, and caffeine shortens it. Candidate mechanism for delayed synaptic recovery after repolarisation.
- Jørgensen et al. (2020). Neural dysfunction correlates with heat coma and CTmax in Drosophila but does not set the boundaries for heat stress survival. *Journal of Experimental Biology* jeb.218750. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.218750 [FT]  
  Heat coma and loss of coordination in five Drosophila species coincide with CNS SD events (heat coma at ~38.7–45.1 °C across species), but selective head heating shows CNS failure is not the proximate cause of heat death. Source for heat-induced SD; caution against equating SD with death.
- Robertson et al. (2020). Neural shutdown under stress: an evolutionary perspective on spreading depolarization. *Journal of Neurophysiology* 123, 885-895. https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00724.2019 [Rec]  
  Evolutionary perspective on SD as a protective neural shutdown in insects vs pathological in mammals; key framing for the limits of cross-species transfer.
- Cheslock et al. (2021). Thermal acclimation alters Na+/K+-ATPase activity in a tissue-specific manner in Drosophila melanogaster. *Comparative Biochemistry and Physiology Part A: Molecular & Integrative Physiology* 256, 110934. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cbpa.2021.110934 [Abs]  
  Tissue-specific Na+/K+-ATPase activity in warm vs cold-acclimated flies; cold acclimation lowers head (brain) pump activity. Context for pump-capacity parameters.
- Robertson & Van Dusen (2021). Motor patterning, ion regulation and spreading depolarization during CNS shutdown induced by experimental anoxia in Locusta migratoria. *Comparative Biochemistry and Physiology Part A: Molecular & Integrative Physiology* 260, 111022. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cbpa.2021.111022 [Abs]  
  Compares N2, CO2 and water-immersion anoxia in locusts: CO2 hastens SD and delays recovery via interstitial acidosis; describes a post-anoxic negativity. Method-dependence of onset/recovery timing.
- Andersen et al. (2022). Plasticity in Na+/K+-ATPase thermal kinetics drives variation in the temperature of cold-induced neural shutdown of adult Drosophila melanogaster. *Journal of Experimental Biology* 225, jeb244923. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.244923 [Abs]  
  Ouabain-induced SD latency in flies follows a thermal performance curve, and cold acclimation shifts it by changing Na+/K+-ATPase thermal sensitivity rather than abundance. Links pump kinetics directly to SD threshold.
- Robertson et al. (2023). A cold and quiet brain: mechanisms of insect CNS arrest at low temperatures. *Current Opinion in Insect Science* 58, 101055. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cois.2023.101055 [Abs]  
  Review of insect CNS arrest in the cold: SD as an off switch, modulated by Kv channels, Na+/K+-ATPase and NKCC and by octopamine.
- Wang et al. (2024). Palytoxin evokes reversible spreading depolarization in the locust CNS. *Journal of Neurophysiology* 132, 1621-1632. https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00251.2024 [Abs]  
  Palytoxin, which converts Na+/K+-ATPase into a cation channel, triggers rapid, reversible SD in locust CNS; supports pump-to-channel conversion as a candidate SD trigger.
- Andersen et al. (2025). Temperature sensitivity of spreading depolarization in the CNS of Drosophila melanogaster. *American Journal of Physiology-Regulatory, Integrative and Comparative Physiology* 329, R134-R149. https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00040.2025 [FT]  
  Quantifies temperature dependence of fly SD: propagation ~2.5 mm/min at room temperature with Q10 ≈ 3.0 (warm-acclimated) or 2.1 (cold-acclimated), and lower temperature delays anoxic SD. Supplies Q10 values for the hypothermia arm (H4).
- Robertson & Wang (2025). Recovery from spreading depolarization is slowed by aging and accelerated by antioxidant treatment in locusts. *Journal of Neurophysiology* 133, 245-256. https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00487.2024 [Abs]  
  In locusts, ageing prolongs SD events and slows DC recovery, while an antioxidant does the opposite; ouabain also produces a slow, permanent negative ultraslow potential. Evidence for cumulative oxidative damage limiting recovery.
- Robertson et al. (2025). Impairment of the blood-brain barrier accelerates a negative ultraslow potential in the locust CNS. *Journal of Neurophysiology* 134, 471-485. https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00191.2025 [Abs]  
  Blood–brain-barrier impairment accelerates the negative ultraslow potential (a marker of accumulating damage) without changing SD timing in locusts: fast SD dynamics run on top of slow injury. Motivates separate fast (SD) and slow (damage) state variables.
- Andersen et al. (2026). Voltage-gated K+ channels modulate the neural abiotic stress tolerance of Drosophila melanogaster. *Comparative Biochemistry and Physiology Part A: Molecular & Integrative Physiology* 313, 111969. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cbpa.2026.111969 [Abs]  
  Glial voltage-gated K+ channels (sei, Shaw) set CNS tolerance to cold, anoxia and heat SD in Drosophila, implicating glial K+ buffering. Supports a glial buffering term in the simulator.

## B. Mammalian ischaemia, spreading depolarization, reperfusion and hypothermia

- Lowry et al. (1964). Effect of Ischemia on Known Substrates and Cofactors of the Glycolytic Pathway in Brain. *Journal of Biological Chemistry* 239, 18-30. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0021-9258(18)51740-3 [Abs]  
  Time course of glycolytic substrates, phosphocreatine and adenine nucleotides in mouse brain after decapitation; ischaemia raised glycolytic flux 4–7-fold. Basis for energy-reserve depletion kinetics (exact time constants not extracted).
- Hossmann & Kleihues (1973). Reversibility of Ischemic Brain Damage. *Archives of Neurology* 29, 375-384. https://doi.org/10.1001/archneur.1973.00490300037004 [Rec]  
  Early experimental evidence that neuronal function can recover after prolonged complete ischaemia in cats, questioning the 'few minutes' rule. Full text not retrieved.
- Hossmann & Hossmann (1973). Return of neuronal functions after prolonged cardiac arrest. *Brain Research* 60, 423-438. https://doi.org/10.1016/0006-8993(73)90800-7 [Rec]  
  Report of return of neuronal function after prolonged cardiac arrest in animals; historical evidence that irreversibility windows depend on reperfusion conditions. Full text not retrieved.
- Astrup et al. (1981). Thresholds in cerebral ischemia - the ischemic penumbra.. *Stroke* 12, 723-725. https://doi.org/10.1161/01.STR.12.6.723 [Rec]  
  Defines the ischaemic penumbra between thresholds of electrical failure and ion-pump failure. Full text not retrieved; thresholds taken from Hossmann 1994 instead.
- Hansen (1985). Effect of anoxia on ion distribution in the brain. *Physiological Reviews* 65, 101-148. https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.101 [Rec]  
  Classic review of ion redistribution in anoxic brain (K+ efflux, Na+/Ca2+ influx at anoxic depolarisation). Full text not retrieved; numeric values from it are not used here as verified.
- CHOI (1988). Glutamate neurotoxicity and diseases of the nervous system. *Neuron* 1, 623-634. https://doi.org/10.1016/0896-6273(88)90162-6 [Rec]  
  Foundational account of glutamate excitotoxicity; justifies an activity-dependent damage term during reperfusion surges.
- Erecińska & Silver (1989). ATP and Brain Function. *Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow & Metabolism* 9, 2-19. https://doi.org/10.1038/jcbfm.1989.2 [Rec]  
  Review of ATP and brain function, energy production and consumption; background for ATP-dependent pump modelling.
- Hossmann (1994). Viability thresholds and the penumbra of focal ischemia. *Annals of Neurology* 36, 557-565. https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.410360404 [Abs]  
  Hierarchy of blood-flow thresholds: protein synthesis stops ~0.55, anaerobic glycolysis ~0.35, energy failure ~0.20, anoxic depolarisation <0.15 ml/g/min; penumbra viability is time-limited. Supplies graded-insult thresholds. Imaging shows the core expanding into the penumbra over a few hours, attributed to peri-infarct depolarisations.
- Lipton (1999). Ischemic Cell Death in Brain Neurons. *Physiological Reviews* 79, 1431-1568. https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1999.79.4.1431 [Abs]  
  Comprehensive review of mechanisms of ischaemic neuronal death (excitotoxicity, Ca2+ overload, delayed death). Background for damage-accumulation rules.
- Ames (2000). CNS energy metabolism as related to function. *Brain Research Reviews* 34, 42-68. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0165-0173(00)00038-2 [Abs]  
  Review relating CNS energy use to function: most brain energy goes to signalling, particularly Na+ transport, and reserves and safety margins are small. Supports pump-dominated ATP demand qualitatively; the often-quoted ~50% pump share is not stated in the abstract and remains unverified.
- HACA Group (2002). Mild Therapeutic Hypothermia to Improve the Neurologic Outcome after Cardiac Arrest. *New England Journal of Medicine* 346, 549-556. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa012689 [Abs]  
  RCT: 32–34 °C for 24 h after VF cardiac arrest raised favourable neurological outcome (55% vs 39%) and lowered 6-month mortality (41% vs 55%). Clinical evidence for H4.
- Bernard et al. (2002). Treatment of Comatose Survivors of Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest with Induced Hypothermia. *New England Journal of Medicine* 346, 557-563. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa003289 [Abs]  
  RCT (n = 77): 33 °C for 12 h after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest gave 49% vs 26% good outcome (adjusted OR 5.25). Corroborates HACA for H4.
- Erecinska et al. (2003). Effects of Hypothermia on Energy Metabolism in Mammalian Central Nervous System. *Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow & Metabolism* 23, 513-530. https://doi.org/10.1097/01.WCB.0000066287.21705.21 [Abs]  
  Review: hypothermia lowers cerebral glucose/O2 consumption, slows ATP and phosphocreatine breakdown during hypoxia–ischaemia and improves energetic recovery. Mechanistic basis for H4.
- Nilsson & Lutz (2004). Anoxia Tolerant Brains. *Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow & Metabolism* 24, 475-486. https://doi.org/10.1097/00004647-200405000-00001 [Abs]  
  Review of anoxia-tolerant vertebrate brains (turtle, crucian carp) that survive by metabolic depression and channel arrest; comparative benchmark for fly tolerance.
- Saver (2006). Time Is Brain—Quantified. *Stroke* 37, 263-266. https://doi.org/10.1161/01.STR.0000196957.55928.ab [Abs]  
  Estimates ~1.9 million neurons lost per minute in untreated large-vessel stroke, with infarct evolution over ~10 h (6–18 h). Scaling of loss rate.
- Gao et al. (2008). Protective effects of ischemic postconditioning compared with gradual reperfusion or preconditioning. *Journal of Neuroscience Research* 86, 2505-2511. https://doi.org/10.1002/jnr.21703 [Abs]  
  In rat focal ischaemia, gradual reperfusion and specific postconditioning schedules (3×30 s/10 s; 10×10 s/10 s) reduced infarct size, but protection was lost when started 3 min after reperfusion. Direct mammalian evidence for H3.
- Zhao (2009). Ischemic Postconditioning as a Novel Avenue to Protect against Brain Injury after Stroke. *Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow & Metabolism* 29, 873-885. https://doi.org/10.1038/jcbfm.2009.13 [Abs]  
  Review of cerebral ischaemic postconditioning (stuttering reperfusion), noting reported protection when applied up to 6 h after focal ischaemia. Context for staged reperfusion.
- Dreier (2011). The role of spreading depression, spreading depolarization and spreading ischemia in neurological disease. *Nature Medicine* 17, 439-447. https://doi.org/10.1038/nm.2333 [Abs]  
  Review of SD, spreading depression and spreading ischaemia in human neurological disease; lists triggers including pump inhibitors, hypoxia and ischaemia.
- Eltzschig & Eckle (2011). Ischemia and reperfusion—from mechanism to translation. *Nature Medicine* 17, 1391-1401. https://doi.org/10.1038/nm.2507 [Abs]  
  Review of ischaemia–reperfusion injury mechanisms and translation; supports the premise that reperfusion itself can injure.
- Dreier & Reiffurth (2015). The Stroke-Migraine Depolarization Continuum. *Neuron* 86, 902-922. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.04.004 [Rec]  
  Places migraine aura SD and ischaemic terminal depolarisation on a single continuum; framework for graded insult severity.
- Hartings et al. (2016). The continuum of spreading depolarizations in acute cortical lesion development: Examining Leão’s legacy. *Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow & Metabolism* 37, 1571-1594. https://doi.org/10.1177/0271678X16654495 [Abs]  
  Continuum of spreading depolarisations in lesion development: persistent depolarisation defines the ischaemic core, while transient SDs in the penumbra progressively expand it.
- Dreier et al. (2018). Terminal spreading depolarization and electrical silence in death of human cerebral cortex. *Annals of Neurology* 83, 295-310. https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.25147 [Abs]  
  Human recordings during dying: non-spreading depression at brain tissue pO2 ~11 mmHg, then terminal SD 3.9 min (IQR 2.6–6.3) after the final perfusion drop; anoxic depolarisation typically starts 2–5 min after severe ischaemia. Anchor for mammalian timing.

## C. Brain death criteria and irreversibility

- Greer et al. (2020). Determination of Brain Death/Death by Neurologic Criteria. *JAMA* 324, 1078. https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2020.11586 [Abs]  
  World Brain Death Project international consensus: BD/DNC requires coma, brainstem areflexia and apnoea (no breathing at pH < 7.30 and PaCO2 ≥ 60 mmHg), after excluding confounders; ancillary tests when the exam cannot be completed. Gives operational components that the model's 'brain death' analogue mirrors (unresponsiveness plus absent intrinsic drive).
- Gligorov (2023). Is Death Irreversible?. *The Journal of Medicine and Philosophy: A Forum for Bioethics and Philosophy of Medicine* 48, 492-503. https://doi.org/10.1093/jmp/jhad027 [Abs]  
  Philosophical analysis arguing irreversibility remains part of the definition of death, indexed to current possibilities of reversal. Frames what 'resuscitation' of a brain-dead state would have to show.
- Greer et al. (2023). Pediatric and Adult Brain Death/Death by Neurologic Criteria Consensus Guideline. *Neurology* 101, 1112-1132. https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000207740 [Abs]  
  2023 AAN/AAP/CNS/SCCM consensus guideline for brain death/death by neurologic criteria (85 recommendations: prerequisites, examination, apnoea test, ancillary tests, ECMO/TTM). Defines the clinical construct the simulation's 'brain death' state is compared against.

## D. Post-mortem restoration (BrainEx / OrganEx) and follow-ups

- Farahany et al. (2019). Part-revived pig brains raise slew of ethical quandaries. *Nature* 568, 299-302. https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-019-01168-9 [Rec]  
  Ethics commentary on BrainEx raising questions about consciousness, death definitions and research oversight.
- Vrselja et al. (2019). Restoration of brain circulation and cellular functions hours post-mortem. *Nature* 568, 336-343. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1099-1 [FT]  
  BrainEx: pig brains perfused ex vivo for 6 h starting 4 h post-mortem showed preserved cytoarchitecture, reduced cell death, restored vascular/glial responses, spontaneous synaptic activity and metabolism, but no global electrocorticographic activity. Central evidence that cellular recovery and network function can dissociate (H5).
- Andrijevic et al. (2022). Cellular recovery after prolonged warm ischaemia of the whole body. *Nature* 608, 405-412. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-05016-1 [Abs]  
  OrganEx: whole-body pig perfusion after 1 h warm ischaemia preserved tissue integrity, reduced cell death and restored selected cellular processes across organs, with snRNA-seq of repair programs; no organised brain activity was reported.
- Long & Resnik (2025). Precautionary Principle and Post-Mortem Brain Resuscitation. *Neuroethics* 18, 32. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12152-025-09605-5 [Abs]  
  2025 neuroethics paper proposing a precautionary principle for post-mortem brain resuscitation research. Recent follow-up (ethics, not primary data).
- Remondelli et al. (2026). Emergency preservation and resuscitation in exsanguination cardiac arrest: science fiction to future reality?. *Trauma Surgery & Acute Care Open* 11, e002165. https://doi.org/10.1136/tsaco-2025-002165 [Abs]  
  2026 review of emergency preservation and resuscitation via profound hypothermia, discussing BrainEx/OrganEx as adjunct platforms. Recent follow-up (review).

> **Follow-up status (2023–2026):** within this search boundary no new peer-reviewed *primary* BrainEx/OrganEx data paper was located. Follow-ups found are ethics/review articles (Gligorov 2023; Long 2025; Remondelli 2026) and a 2023 Yale dissertation reporting optimised BrainEx perfusion up to 24 h with neuronal tracer uptake (dissertation, not peer reviewed; not counted). A commercial human-brain perfusion platform has been described in the press with results available only as conference posters (not peer reviewed; not counted).

## E. Connectomes and whole-brain connectome models

- Zheng et al. (2018). A Complete Electron Microscopy Volume of the Brain of Adult Drosophila melanogaster. *Cell* 174, 730-743.e22. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2018.06.019 [Rec]  
  FAFB serial-section EM volume of the whole adult female fly brain underlying FlyWire.
- Azevedo et al. (2024). Connectomic reconstruction of a female Drosophila ventral nerve cord. *Nature* 631, 360-368. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07389-x [Rec]  
  FANC: connectomic reconstruction of a female VNC; additional VNC reference for cross-dataset comparisons.
- Dorkenwald et al. (2024). Neuronal wiring diagram of an adult brain. *Nature* 634, 124-138. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07558-y [Abs]  
  FlyWire whole-brain connectome of an adult female (139,255 neurons, ~5×10^7 synapses) with cell classes, projectome and sensory-to-motor information-flow analysis. FAFB v783 data source.
- Lappalainen et al. (2024). Connectome-constrained networks predict neural activity across the fly visual system. *Nature* 634, 1132-1140. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07939-3 [Abs]  
  Connectome-constrained deep mechanistic networks predict neural activity across the fly visual system. Alternative modelling approach and validation precedent.
- Marin et al. (2024). Systematic annotation of a complete adult male Drosophila nerve cord connectome reveals principles of functional organisation. **. https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.97766 [Rec]  
  Systematic annotation of MANC neurons (hemilineages, cell types). Needed to define VNC sensory inputs and motor readouts.
- Schlegel et al. (2024). Whole-brain annotation and multi-connectome cell typing of Drosophila. *Nature* 634, 139-152. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07686-5 [Rec]  
  Whole-brain annotation and cross-connectome cell typing (FlyWire vs hemibrain), quantifying stereotypy and variability. Basis for matching neuron identities across datasets (H6).
- Shiu et al. (2024). A Drosophila computational brain model reveals sensorimotor processing. *Nature* 634, 210-219. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07763-9 [FT]  
  Whole-brain LIF model of FlyWire (Brian2) with connectome weights and predicted transmitter signs; one free parameter (Wsyn = 0.275 mV) and Poisson sensory drive predicted feeding and grooming sensorimotor pathways that were validated experimentally. The template simulator for this project.
- Takemura et al. (2024). A Connectome of the Male Drosophila Ventral Nerve Cord. **. https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.97769 [Rec]  
  MANC: connectome of the male adult ventral nerve cord. Dataset for H6 (VNC replication).
- Nern et al. (2025). Connectome-driven neural inventory of a complete visual system. *Nature* 641, 1225-1237. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08746-0 [Rec]  
  MAOL: connectome-driven inventory of the complete male right optic lobe visual system.
- Stürner et al. (2025). Comparative connectomics of Drosophila descending and ascending neurons. *Nature* 643, 158-172. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08925-z [Rec]  
  Comparative connectomics of descending and ascending neurons across FAFB, MANC and FANC; the brain–cord interface relevant to sensorimotor reachability.
- Bates et al. (2026). Distributed control circuits across a brain-and-cord connectome. *Nature* 656, 957-970. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10735-w [Abs]  
  BANC: densely reconstructed female brain-and-nerve-cord connectome showing local sensory–effector loops linked by ascending/descending modules. Dataset for H6 (CNS replication).
- Berg et al. (2026). Sexual dimorphism in the complete Drosophila male central nervous system connectome. *Cell* 189, 5504-5526.e15. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2026.08.015 [Abs]  
  MCNS: complete male CNS connectome (166,700 neurons, 11,710 types) compared with female brain; MCNS v1.0 is the substrate of the Xenova browser simulation. Dataset for H6.

## F. Neural energy budgets and metabolic scaling

- Laughlin et al. (1998). The metabolic cost of neural information. *Nature Neuroscience* 1, 36-41. https://doi.org/10.1038/236 [Rec]  
  Measured ATP cost per bit in blowfly photoreceptors and interneurons; empirical insect evidence that neural information is metabolically expensive.
- Attwell & Laughlin (2001). An Energy Budget for Signaling in the Grey Matter of the Brain. *Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow & Metabolism* 21, 1133-1145. https://doi.org/10.1097/00004647-200110000-00001 [Abs]  
  Bottom-up energy budget for rodent grey matter: action potentials 47%, postsynaptic glutamate 34%, resting potential 13%, recycling 3% of signalling energy; predicts ≤15% of neurons active at once. Basis for activity-dependent ATP demand.
- Gillooly et al. (2001). Effects of Size and Temperature on Metabolic Rate. *Science* 293, 2248-2251. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1061967 [Abs]  
  General model of metabolic rate scaling with body mass and temperature (Boltzmann factor). Used only to derive an approximate metabolic Q10, flagged as derived.
- Niven & Laughlin (2008). Energy limitation as a selective pressure on the evolution of sensory systems. *Journal of Experimental Biology* 211, 1792-1804. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.017574 [Abs]  
  Review of energy as a selective pressure on sensory systems: high resting and signalling costs, mostly via Na+/K+-ATPase, with diminishing returns for capacity. Supports scaling ATP demand with membrane area and channel load.
- Sengupta et al. (2010). Action Potential Energy Efficiency Varies Among Neuron Types in Vertebrates and Invertebrates. *PLoS Computational Biology* 6, e1000840. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1000840 [Abs]  
  Action-potential energy cost varies >10-fold among neuron types depending on Na+/K+ current overlap; cautions that size alone does not fix cost.
- Harris et al. (2012). Synaptic Energy Use and Supply. *Neuron* 75, 762-777. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2012.08.019 [Rec]  
  Review of synaptic energy use and supply (mitochondria, vesicle cycling); supports per-synapse ATP cost scaling.
- Howarth et al. (2012). Updated Energy Budgets for Neural Computation in the Neocortex and Cerebellum. *Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow & Metabolism* 32, 1222-1232. https://doi.org/10.1038/jcbfm.2012.35 [Abs]  
  Updated budgets with efficient APs: cortex signalling energy 50% postsynaptic receptors, 21% APs, 20% resting potentials; cerebellum dominated by resting potentials (54%). Alternative weighting of rest vs activity cost.

## G. Network robustness / percolation theory

- Albert et al. (2000). Error and attack tolerance of complex networks. *Nature* 406, 378-382. https://doi.org/10.1038/35019019 [Rec]  
  Scale-free networks tolerate random node removal but fragment rapidly under targeted hub removal. Theoretical basis for H2 (targeted vs random loss).
- Callaway et al. (2000). Network Robustness and Fragility: Percolation on Random Graphs. *Physical Review Letters* 85, 5468-5471. https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.85.5468 [Rec]  
  Percolation theory of network robustness under random and degree-targeted removal, giving critical thresholds for giant-component collapse. Theoretical basis for H1.
